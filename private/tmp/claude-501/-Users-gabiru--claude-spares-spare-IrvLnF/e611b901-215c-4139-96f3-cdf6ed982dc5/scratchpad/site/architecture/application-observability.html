Application Observability Architecture#

How Heartbeat observes OutSystems applications: where their telemetry comes from, which fields matter, the metrics derived from it and how requests are correlated across logs. The decision behind the ingest path is ADR 0008; the operator flow is in workflows.

Status: partly built. Extraction to the OTel Collector and the derived metrics (data path steps 1, 2, 4 and 5) run as the db-collector's outsystems_logs kind, switched off by default (configuration); the chart ships their recording rules and SLO alerts. Loki indexes outsystems.log_type as a label (outsystems_log_type in LogQL). Request traces are designed, not built. Field names and log types follow OutSystems 11; they are confirmed against the SRE team's queries and the logging database DDL before the queries are reviewed.

Scope#

Deployment Ingest path Status
OutSystems 11 self-managed (Traditional and Reactive) The db-collector's outsystems_logs kind reads the oslog_* tables of the logging database and sends OTLP logs to the OTel Collector First, phase 2
OutSystems 11 on OutSystems Cloud Log streaming add-on sends OTLP straight to the OTel Collector When needed
ODC App Analytics Stream sends OTLP straight to the OTel Collector When needed

Data path for self-managed OutSystems 11:

  1. The collector runs each enabled log-type query (one .sql file per log type, see the query contract) for each logging-database target, from its watermark to now minus a lag, in batches of at most batch_limit rows and about 8 MiB of row data.

  2. Each row becomes an OTLP log record: Instant is the timestamp, Message the body, every other column an attribute under its OutSystems name. The collector adds error.fingerprint to error records. Values longer than 16 KiB, mostly Stack and EnvironmentInformation, are cut and the record is marked outsystems.truncated.

  3. Records go to the OTel Collector, then to Loki. Loki indexes only service.name, deployment.environment.name and outsystems.log_type (service.namespace too, which the collector does not set). The records hold personal data, so Loki deletes them after 30 days (loki.loki.limits_config.retention_period; the compactor's delete_request_store must name the store that holds the chunks: filesystem on kind, s3 in production). Compliance confirms the period before the first production deploy (ADR 0012).

  4. From the same rows the collector updates the derived metrics, which Prometheus scrapes from its /metrics.

  5. The watermark moves only after the batch is delivered: accepted by the OTel Collector, whose logs exporter sends batches of at most 4 MiB to Loki one at a time, in order, and retries a throttled one until Loki takes it (a push above Loki's 6 MB burst is never taken). Loki rejects for good a row more than an hour behind the newest row of its stream, so batches sent out of order lose rows. The logs pipeline has no batch processor, which would accept rows before they are queued.

    The exporter's queue holds one request, on disk (the otel-collector-queue volume), and the receiver waits for room in it. A larger queue does not keep its order across a Collector restart: it sends the requests it was sending when it stopped after the rest, and with 64 MiB a restart during a three-hour backlog lost 1,297 of 6,000 rows in the kind test. With one request nothing queues behind the request being sent, and the disk keeps that request across the restart: the kind test restarts the Collector during backlogs of 45 minutes and three hours, with Loki down, and Loki ends with every row of both, once.

    So the Collector takes the next batch only once Loki has the one before, and while Loki is down it takes none. The collector waits for it without treating it as a failure: the export runs in the background, outside the probe deadline, and is retried until the Collector accepts it; meanwhile the log type reads nothing more, and heartbeat_outsystems_export_blocked is 1 (OutSystemsLogExportBlocked fires after 2 minutes). The target stays up and does not back off, so it resumes as soon as the Collector accepts the batch. The rows were read and the query closed before the export started, so nothing is held on the logging database while it waits, and the batches held in memory are bounded (64 MiB of row data). A backlog drains at about the rate Loki takes it (throughput).

Export throughput#

Measured on kind with one log type of 12,000 error rows of about 6 KB (72 MB) spanning three hours, polled every 10 s:

Queue Backlog with Loki up Backlog after a Loki outage
One request (this chart) 85 s, 87 s, 102 s, 105 s 61 s after Loki was back; the target stayed up while it waited
64 MiB (earlier charts) 108 s, 177 s The queue filled, the target failed and backed off, and 15 minutes after Loki was back it held 12,339 lines for the 12,000 rows: batches sent again after 503s

Loki's ingestion rate limit sets the pace either way: the Collector pushes 4 MiB at a time and waits at least 5 s after a throttled push. The 64 MiB queue only moved the wait from the collector into the Collector. Other OTLP sources of logs, none yet, wait the same way; one that gives up sooner (an OpenTelemetry SDK retries for about a minute) drops its batch, where a larger queue would have held it.

The mapping from each view's own columns to these names is the standard columns table of the queries README.

Identifiers are never Loki labels or metric labels. They stay structured metadata, queryable with | User_Id="…". They are kept raw, with access to Loki and Grafana restricted, and production needs compliance sign-off first (ADR 0012); the extractor can hash them before export without changing anything else.

Derived metrics#

The collector computes these from every row it delivers to the OTel Collector and exposes them on its /metrics (code). They are counted when the row is extracted, so they trail real time by the lag plus the poll interval (about a minute by default), and a batch delivered twice (a crash between delivery and the watermark save) is counted twice.

Every metric has the labels collector, environment and application (the record's service.name), plus those shown. Labels are capped so a name the platform invents cannot grow the series without bound: the first 100 values of module per application and of operation per application and log type keep their own series, later ones are counted as other; front_end is capped at 50 and integration type at 20 per collector, application at 1,000. The kept values are the first seen since the collector started, so a restart can keep different ones. The caps multiply, so each collector also holds at most max_derived_series series of these metrics; past it a row that needs a new series is left out of that metric and counted, and OutSystemsDerivedSeriesOverflow fires (series budget). User, session, request and IP never become labels.

Entry points are the rows that stand for one request or timer run: screen (Traditional and Reactive screens; operation is the screen), mobile_request (Reactive server calls; the endpoint), cyclic_job (the timer) and integration rows of exposed APIs (the API action).

Not built, on purpose#

  • Active users and sessions as gauges. A distinct count needs every identifier of the window in the collector's memory; it resets on a restart and would double when collectors run as replicas (phase 8). Loki counts them correctly from the structured metadata already sent, so dashboards query Loki instead. A LogQL distinct count holds one series per ID, so the chart raises Loki's max_query_series from 500 to 20000.
  • New error fingerprints. "Never seen before" needs a persisted, bounded set of fingerprints, or every restart reports all errors as new and looks like a bad deployment. It waits until a dashboard or alert needs it; until then error.fingerprint is on every error record in Loki.

SLOs and alerts#

Recording rules in heartbeat.rules.yml turn the metrics into SLIs per environment and application, over user-facing requests (screens, Reactive server calls and exposed APIs; timers are left out):

  • Availability: the share of requests with outcome="success" (heartbeat:outsystems_request_errors:ratio_rate<window>).
  • Latency: the share of requests answered within 2 s, from the histogram's le="2" bucket (heartbeat:outsystems_request_slow:ratio_rate<window>).

Alerts use multiwindow burn rates against static default objectives, 99.9% available (a 0.1% error budget) and 99% within 2 s over 30 days: fast burn (14.4× over 1h and 5m, an error ratio above 1.44%) is critical, slow burn (6× over 6h and 30m, above 0.6%) is warning. Low-traffic applications are left out so a handful of failures cannot page: the availability alerts need five requests a minute over the long window, so a fast burn takes at least 5 failed requests in the hour; the latency alerts need one. Because the SLIs go quiet, not red, when extraction stops, OutSystemsLogExtractionBehind fires when a log type's extraction lag passes 30 minutes, and OutSystemsLogExportBlocked sooner, 2 minutes after the OTel Collector stops taking batches, as it does while Loki is down. These are guardrails; per-application objectives and adaptive baselines come in phase 7.

Dashboards#

Four dashboards in Grafana's Heartbeat folder read these metrics and the log records (files/grafana/dashboards/). Each one filters by environment and application. A header link opens the others with the same filters and time range, and another opens the SQL Server Overview for the same time.

Dashboard What it answers From
OutSystems Overview (outsystems-overview) Are users served well right now: requests, failed and slow shares against the objectives, p95, error-budget burn, active users and sessions, extraction lag; then the busiest operations, front-end skew, integrations, slow SQL, timers and Heartbeat's own extraction Metrics; users and sessions from Loki
OutSystems Errors (outsystems-errors) What fails, where and for whom: errors by module and front-end, failed operations, the top fingerprints, users affected, and the error log Metrics and Loki

Drill-down goes from the overview's top operations to a module's latency or errors, then from any Request_Key in a table to the request timeline. A fingerprint in the top errors filters the error log to that error. The metric panels trail real time by the extraction delay (about a minute); the Loki panels show rows as soon as they are in Loki. A Go test checks every dashboard's datasources, variables and links (tests/dashboards_test.go); the metric-reference test covers the metric names.

Request traces#

The gap#

APMs such as Dynatrace instrument the .NET processes behind OutSystems: they see IIS requests, ASP.NET handlers and SQL statements, but not which OutSystems application, module, screen or action they belong to, and OutSystems 11 logs carry no trace ID to join them by. The result is traces that are hard to map to the platform's own concepts.

OutSystems already ties its own logs together: every row a request writes carries the same Request_Key, across log types and modules. Heartbeat can build request timelines from that key, in OutSystems terms, without any agent.

Step A: request timeline from logs (with phase 2)#

  • Request_Key is kept as structured metadata on every record.
  • A Grafana dashboard shows one request across log types: entry point (screen, server call, timer or exposed API) with its duration, the integrations and extensions it called, slow SQL, and errors, ordered by time.
  • Investigations filter by user, session or IP, then open the requests they find.

This needs no new storage and proves whether Request_Key is reliably present in each log type. The OutSystems Request Timeline dashboard does both (dashboards); its Rows carrying a Request_Key panel is the measurement, which needs a real installation's logs to mean anything (PatientZero writes the key on every row). scripts/outsystems-log-queries.sql holds the SQL the SRE team runs against the logging database for the same timelines.

Step B: synthesized OTLP traces (after step A)#

The extractor turns rows into spans as well as log records:

Span From
Trace ID A hash of Request_Key, so the logs and the trace link both ways without storing a mapping
Root span The entry-point row: screen, server call, timer or exposed API, with its duration
Child spans Integration, extension and slow-SQL rows with the same Request_Key, timed from Instant and Duration (Instant is the end for integrations and mobile requests, the start for screens and timers)
Span events Error rows
Attributes Application, module, operation, front-end, outcome; identifiers as on the logs
Span links Calls from one OutSystems app to another one's exposed API, matched by endpoint and time; marked as inferred

The OTel Collector's spanmetrics connector can then produce the same RED metrics from spans, and Tempo, the store Heartbeat's own traces already use (ADR 0018), shows waterfalls with links to the logs and the SQL Server dashboards for the same time. Step B is not part of phase 2's exit.

Limits, stated in the product: spans exist only where OutSystems logs something. Fast SQL, internal actions and client-side time do not appear, and timings are as precise as the platform's millisecond durations. That covers what SREs triage first (which request, which module, which dependency) without replacing a code-level profiler.

Not recommended now: OTel .NET auto-instrumentation on the front-end servers. It needs an agent on the platform servers and produces the same unlabelled .NET spans an APM does.

Open questions#

Answered from the OutSystems 11 documentation (Log data reference, The log tables and views):

  • Is Instant the start or the end of the logged operation? Per log type: the start for screen and cyclic_job, the completion for integration and mobile_request, the time of logging for error, general and extension, the time of the event for request_event. The start-stamped rows are written when the operation ends, hence the per-log- type lag (ADR 0008).
  • Which time zone? The platform servers' own. OutSystems Cloud runs UTC; a self-managed installation uses whatever its front-ends and database share. log_timezone is set per collector.
  • Which log types carry Request_Key? All eight current ones: general, error (Request_key), screen, integration, extension, mobile request, cyclic job and request event (REQUESTKEY). Only the deprecated web reference, web service and SMS logs and the detail tables lack it.
  • Retention. Ten weekly cycle tables per log type; the oslog_<TYPE> view is the current week, oslog_<TYPE>_Previous the week before, switched every Friday at 23:45 database time. The retention period is set in the Configuration Tool (Log tab, Advanced settings); OutSystems Cloud keeps 9 weeks, 7 with log separation. initial_lookback can reach back one week at most, since the queries read two weeks' views.

scripts/outsystems-log-survey.sql (read-only) ran against one installation's logging database on 2026-10-19. Its native tables were empty and its data a flattened one-week extract, so it answered some questions and left others open:

  • Schema: answered. The test fixtures are its DDL: varchar text, datetime2 instants, Error_Id as text in every log (empty for none), the error log's EnvironmentInformation, and cycle tables clustered on Id or not clustered at all, so rows that share an Instant come back in no particular order. The oslog_Error view also joins oslog_Error_Detail for full stack traces.
  • Rows per minute at peak: about 3,800 across screen, integration, extension and slow-SQL rows at the busiest minute of the week, about 1,000 on average; no single log type passed 2,200. The default batch_limit of 5000 covers it.
  • Duration unit: milliseconds for screen, integration and extension (the reference says seconds for extension); maxima sit at timeouts in milliseconds. Still open for mobile request and cyclic job: the survey had none of their rows.
  • Still open: whether every request writes an entry-point row (screen or mobile request), and the installation's time zone (from an Instant next to the database's SYSDATETIMEOFFSET(); a stale copy cannot tell). The hourly curve peaks at 11:00 and bottoms at 04:00, which reads as local time.
