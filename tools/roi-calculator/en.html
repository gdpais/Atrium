How the numbers are worked out

The step times, the share of releases with a regression and the hours to fix are estimates, not measurements. Before quoting a figure to a client, time two or three real diagnoses by hand and with Atrium, and run the release comparison over past releases in a pilot, then enter those numbers.

  • Manual diagnosis. A developer works through the Service Center reports (slow screens and actions, slow SQL, errors), exports them, links each slow query back to the action that ran it, checks the query plan, often with a DBA, and writes up the finding.
  • Automated. Atrium's performance analysis opens on an action and lists the queries it ran with their cost, next to the application's latency, errors and logs. The slowest manual step takes minutes. Atrium also shows SQL Server waits, blocking, I/O and memory in the same time window, so it is clear whether the problem is the query or the server; the developer still reads the execution plan and writes the cause and the recommendation.
  • Simplified and Advanced use the same model. They differ only in the cost of an hour. Simplified: gross salary ÷ productive hours, which leaves out what the company pays on top of the salary, so its savings are always a floor. Advanced: gross salary × (1 + employer social security) + other employer costs, the full cost to the company.
  • Productive hours. In Portugal: 52 weeks × 40 h = 2,080 h, minus 22 days of annual leave (176 h), about 11 public holidays that fall on weekdays (88 h) and about 5 days of sick leave and training (40 h), leaves 1,776 h. Taking off about 10% for meetings and admin gives roughly 1,600 h.
  • Diagnoses. (manual minutes − automated minutes) ÷ 60 × diagnoses per developer per month × developers × 12 × hourly value.
  • Regressions. Releases per year × share with a regression × (share caught by Atrium − share caught early today) × (hours to fix late − hours to fix right after release). A regression found late surfaces as an incident, and its diagnosis is already one of the reactive diagnoses, so the diagnosis time saved is taken out once for each of them. No hour is counted twice.
  • Caught early today. Teams with nightly performance tests or close monitoring already catch some regressions before they hurt. Only the extra ones Atrium catches count.
  • Business impact. A user-facing incident lasts at least until it is diagnosed, so a shorter diagnosis shortens it by the same hours: incidents × hours saved per diagnosis × business cost per hour. Only incidents that are among the reactive diagnoses count. It stays at zero until the client enters its own cost per hour; there is no honest default for it.
  • Investment. Net = savings − Atrium cost per year. Return on investment = net ÷ Atrium cost. Break-even team size is the smallest number of developers whose savings cover the cost, with every other figure unchanged.
  • Developer time freed. Hours, and the same hours as full-time equivalents (hours ÷ productive hours). It is capacity handed back to delivery, spread across the team, not a cut in headcount or payroll.
  • Billed rate. For a consultancy, saved hours valued at the billed rate are revenue it can bill for other work, not a cost saved, so the share of team cost is not shown in that case.
  • Measuring the inputs. Releases come from the LifeTime deployment history. The regression share comes from running the release comparison over past releases in the pilot. Hours to fix late come from performance tickets traced back to a release. Compare cost per execution, not totals, so that changes in load don't look like regressions, and compare against the same weekday (7-day shift) as well as the day before.
  • In production. Large companies usually run a commercial APM in production. There Atrium is the analysis and troubleshooting tool beside it: the APM shows that something is slow, Atrium shows which action and which query, and how each release changed them.
  • Not counted. Regressions that never become incidents but pile up into refactoring projects, fewer front-end server scale-ups, and planned performance reviews outside releases.
  • Salary sources (2026). Indeed averages about €38.8k for OutSystems software engineers in Portugal; Payscale tops out near €57k; NextLevelJobs puts the national software median at €32k–40k.
